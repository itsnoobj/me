Someone's across the table from you in a 1:1. They're deciding whether to take the offer from the other team or stay. And they've been listing pros and cons out loud for twenty minutes.

Except — you notice about six minutes in — the list isn't actually doing anything. Every "pro" gets a rebuttal. Every "con" gets a shrug. They're not weighing options. They're narrating a decision they already made, somewhere in the first ten seconds, before a single point went on either side of the list.

<!-- truncate -->

Maybe yours isn't a job offer. Maybe it's whether to finally say the thing in the retro. Whether to let someone go. Whether to ship now or wait another sprint. Doesn't matter — the shape is the same. The pros-and-cons list isn't reasoning. It's theater, performed for an audience of one, so an emotional call can walk around wearing a rational costume.

I don't think this happens because people are bad at logic. I think it happens because the actual decision got made somewhere logic doesn't reach, and by the time the list shows up, it's just there to make the outcome feel earned.

## The same mistake, played twice

Here's the thing I keep noticing: this happens on *both* ends of a decision, not just going in.

Going in, you feel your way toward a choice and call the feeling a value. It's not "I've decided this aligns with what matters to me" — it's "this feels right," and the feeling gets promoted to a principle without anyone checking its credentials. Fear dressed as caution. Ego dressed as ambition. Comfort dressed as loyalty.

Coming out, whatever happens, you retroactively decide whether you were right — based entirely on how it turned out. Good outcome? Great decision, obviously. Bad outcome? Terrible call, should've known better. Except that's a completely different kind of mistake, and somehow nobody clocks that it's happening.

Same move, both times: collapsing an entire process into whatever you happen to be feeling right now. Going in, the feeling is a mood wearing a principle's clothes. Coming out, the feeling is an outcome wearing a verdict's clothes.

Two frameworks, from two completely different fields, independently caught each half of this.

## Going in: is that a principle, or just a mood?

Clearer Thinking has this [Behavioral Leverage Points Diagram](https://www.clearerthinking.org/post/behavioral-leverage-points-to-improve-your-life) — a genuinely dense map of everything that feeds into a decision. Values, relationships, traits, resources, skills, needs, beliefs, goals, principles, plans, habits, environment, emotion — it's a lot, and honestly, most of it is a different post. (It's really a map for "how do I improve my life," and I'm borrowing one corner of it for something narrower.)

The one part worth stealing: in their model, decisions are supposed to be informed by *principles*, and principles are supposed to trace back to *intrinsic values* — the things you care about for their own sake, not because they get you status or money or approval. Not "this feels right." "This is what I'd do if I actually asked myself what I care about here, and gave a straight answer."

That's a completely different question than the one most of us actually ask in the moment. "Does this feel right" is available in about half a second. "Does this trace back to something I actually value" takes work — you have to go find the principle, and check the feeling against it, instead of just letting the feeling stand in for it.

The gap between those two questions is where most bad "aligned" decisions live. Not decisions that were wrong because someone didn't think — decisions that were wrong because someone mistook a mood for a value and never checked.

## Coming out: was that a bad decision, or just a bad outcome?

Annie Duke, in [Thinking in Bets](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/36747489-thinking-in-bets), has a name for the other half of this: **resulting** — judging the quality of a decision by the quality of its outcome, as if the two were the same thing.

They're not. You can make a genuinely good decision — the best call available with the information you had — and still lose, because the world is probabilistic and bad breaks happen to good decisions all the time. You can also make a reckless, poorly reasoned call and get lucky. Poker players learn this fast, because the feedback is immediate and the stakes are real money: the person who went all-in on a coin-flip and won isn't a genius, and the person who made the statistically correct fold and then would've won the hand isn't a fool. The decision and the outcome are just... different things.

Most of us don't operate this cleanly. We look at how something turned out and let that verdict travel backward in time to rewrite whether the decision itself was smart. It's comforting, in a strange way — it means the world is orderly, that good calls get rewarded and bad calls get punished. It's also just wrong, often enough that trusting it will actively make you worse at deciding, because you'll start avoiding good bets that happened to go badly and repeating bad bets that happened to go well.

## Where this actually lands

So here's the throughline: it's the same failure, twice, at opposite ends of the same decision. Once before — mistaking a mood for a principle. Once after — mistaking an outcome for a verdict. Neither mistake is really about intelligence. Both are about substituting whatever you're feeling *right now* for something that requires you to actually stop and check.

Which gives you two questions, not a checklist, not a framework — just two things worth asking, at the two moments they actually matter:

**Before you decide:** is this feeling a principle, or just a mood? Can you name the thing you're actually trying to protect or move toward — or is "this feels right" the whole argument?

**Before you find out how it goes:** if this goes badly, will that make it a bad decision, or just a bad outcome? Say the answer out loud *now*, before you know — because you will not be capable of answering it honestly afterward.

That second one matters more than it sounds like it should. Deciding, in advance, what would make this a good decision regardless of outcome — that's the closest thing to armor against resulting I've found. It doesn't stop bad outcomes from happening. It stops you from lying to yourself about what they meant.

## Back to the 1:1

The person across the table doesn't need a fourteenth pro or con. They need one of two things, and it's usually not obvious which.

Maybe they need to ask whether "I just don't feel ready to leave" is actually a principle — loyalty, or unfinished work they care about — or whether it's just comfort, dressed up to sound like conviction. Or maybe they've already asked that, and what they actually need is permission to decide *before* they know how it turns out, whether a bad outcome six months from now would mean they chose wrong, or just that the dice landed badly on a decision that was fine.

Either way, the list on the table was never going to answer it. The list was never the thing doing the deciding.
